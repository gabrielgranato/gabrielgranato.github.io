abstract: >-
  Can informal customary justice offer a more effective path to building legal
  capacity than formal legal institutions? The answer depends both on whether
  authorities can settle disputes at all and on whether justice provision is
  impartial. Experiences with justice allow citizens to learn about the
  authorities' competence to deliver settlements and also their willingness to
  promote just outcomes, potentially shaping their willingness to finance the
  state. We study these questions through a field experiment in Kananga,
  Democratic Republic of the Congo, randomly assigning registered disputes to free
  arbitration by customary chiefs, state-licensed lawyers, or a control group.
  We compare the two systems' ability to secure lasting settlements and restore
  social relations, using independent assessments of pre-arbitration accounts to
  identify when arbitrators unjustly favor someone from their own ethnic group.
  Cross-randomizations of written documentation and access to higher authorities
  allow us to examine which features contribute to their effectiveness. We then
  examine how experiences with arbitration shape perceptions of authorities'
  competence and impartiality and willingness to finance the state, using changes
  in beliefs and actual property-tax payments.
