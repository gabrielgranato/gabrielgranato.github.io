Can Religion Bridge Ethnic Divides?
Pastors, Chiefs, and a Field Experiment on Collective Action

With Elie Kabue Ngindu.

Abstract

Ethnic fragmentation is often associated with worse economic development. In one prominent mechanism, free-riding in heterogeneous societies undermines public good provision. This paper examines whether religious and ethnic leaders can sustain cooperation in a voluntary public-works program in an ethnically diverse city in the D.R. Congo. Residents are randomly assigned to ethnically homogeneous or mixed work teams that either include an ethnic chief, a pastor, or no leader at all. Chiefs increase work by 6.0 percentage points in homogeneous teams but not in mixed ones, while pastors increase work by 5.8 points in mixed teams but not in homogeneous ones. Heterogeneity by the strength of ethnic attachment explains this reversal. Chiefs still mobilize their coethnics on mixed teams, and on homogeneous teams their effect is concentrated among teams of residents who report a strong ethnic identity. Pastors do increase work on homogeneous teams whose members have weak ethnic attachments, and their effect is largest on mixed teams whose members hold strong but competing ethnic identities. A cross-randomized public disclosure intervention provides further evidence that the chief mobilizes labor contributions through social enforcement while the pastor uses internal incentives. The paper provides the first experimental evidence on how religious leaders can sustain voluntary public-goods provision in fragmented societies.
